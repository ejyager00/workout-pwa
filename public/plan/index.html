      Notes for this phase

      How hard is hard

      Effort is measured by how many more reps you could have done. That number matters more than the weight on the dumbbell.

      EffortWhat it feels like
      5-6Could have done 4 to 6 more reps. Where Phase 1 lives.
      7Could have done 3 more. Phase 2 ceiling.
      8Could have done 2 more. Phase 3 ceiling, and as hard as it ever needs to get.
      9-10Nearly or completely out of reps. Don't go here. It buys almost nothing extra and costs a lot of recovery.

      For cardio, the same 1-10 scale by breathing: 3-4 means you could hold a conversation, 6 means talking is getting choppy, 8 means short phrases only.

      Picking a weight

      Start lighter than you think. Being too light for two weeks costs you nothing. Being too heavy costs you a week of soreness and a lot of enthusiasm.

      • Phase 1: pick a weight you could do about 14 reps with, then stop at 8.
      • Phase 2: a weight where you'd have 3 or 4 reps left at the end of the set.
      • Phase 3: a weight where you'd have 2 reps left at the end of the set.

      Moving up: when you hit the top of the rep range on every set and still had 2 reps in you, take the next dumbbell up next time and start again at the bottom of the range. Legs will climb quickly at first. Pressing will climb slowly. Both are normal.

      Shoulder rules

      Your shoulders move further than most people's, which means the muscles have to do the job that ligaments do for everyone else. The whole upper-body plan is built around that.

      • Palms facing each other whenever there is a choice.
      • On presses, stop when your upper arms reach the line of your torso. Don't chase a deeper stretch.
      • Don't slam your elbows into a locked position at the top of anything.
      • Never skip the face pulls and the external rotations, even on a short day. They are the highest-value thing you do for the joint.

      Not in this program, on purpose: behind-the-neck pressing or pulling, upright rows, bench dips, chest flyes, wide-grip barbell bench, and hanging from a bar.

      Popping and clicking without pain is fine and does not mean damage. Pain, a feeling that the joint wants to slide out of place, or pins and needles down the arm means stop that exercise and get a physical therapist to look at it. One appointment is worth more than any amount of guessing.

      The cardio ramp

      Intervals are introduced slowly and deliberately, and only ever on one day a week. Most people quit interval training because they were told to do 1 minute hard, 1 minute easy before they had the base for it, so effort falls apart by the third round and the whole thing feels like drowning. Long rests early are what prevent that.

      WeeksSession
      1-410 min continuous, easy enough to talk the whole time.
      5-615 min continuous, last 5 min slightly harder.
      7-8One day: 6 x (30 sec harder / 90 sec easy). Other days stay steady.
      9-10One day: 8 x (30 sec harder / 90 sec easy). A second day: 5 x (1 min moderate / 1 min easy).
      11+One hard day: 8 x (40 sec hard / 80 sec easy). One moderate day. One easy steady day.

      Use the bike for intervals. It's seated, low-impact and there's nothing to get wrong when you're breathing hard. Use the treadmill on an incline for the steady days. The elliptical works for either if you prefer it.

      Sticking with it

      • Same three days, same time, on the calendar. Deciding when to go is what kills programs, not the training.
      • The minimum counts. If you're short on time or short on willpower: first two exercises plus five minutes of cardio, then leave. Twelve minutes. Never a zero.
      • Miss a day, do it the next day. Don't stack two sessions together to catch up.
      • Write it down. Weight, reps, how hard it felt. In six weeks the log is the most motivating thing you own.
      • Check in at week 4 and week 10, not continuously. Day-to-day changes are noise.

      What actually changes how you look

      Lifting doesn't burn many calories. Three sessions a week is roughly 700 to 900 for the whole week. Its real job is keeping the muscle you already have, so the weight you lose comes off as fat and you end up looking leaner rather than smaller.

      The rest comes from food and daily movement. A modest calorie deficit, enough protein to hold onto muscle, and a daily step target will each do more for the visible result than anything you could change about the training. Seven or more hours of sleep does more for recovery than any supplement.

      One change at a time, though. Get the three sessions genuinely habitual first. Everything else can wait until it is.
